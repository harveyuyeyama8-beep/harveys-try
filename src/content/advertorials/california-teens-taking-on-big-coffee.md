      Harvey Uyeyama’s first sales plan had a few problems. One was the backpack.

      He sold coffee door to door. One 8-mile trip made just one sale, The Dirt reported.

      “It probably took a hundred nos before my first yes,” he told The Sacramento Bee.

      He got better at what he said at the door. Then he fixed the backpack.

      “I also realized you can’t have coffee in a black backpack. You look like you’re trying to rob them,” he said.

      He switched to a wagon.

      Things got better. The Bee began its story about him with this line: “If you ever want to feel like an underachiever, talk to Harvey Uyeyama.”

      Today, Harvey is a high school senior. He and his co-founder, Abby Merchant, run a business called Harvey’s Coffee.

      Harvey roasts every bag by hand, on a machine built in the early 1900s. Nothing is roasted until someone orders it.

      It is two teens and one old roaster against the whole coffee business. Big warehouses. Trucks. Store shelves.

      That is “Big Coffee.” It is not one company. It is a way of doing business.

      Their bet is simple. Coffee does not wait well. And a shelf is built for waiting.

      Why would a teen sit through a hundred nos? Meet his dad.

  - type: prose
    heading: '“Why don’t you just sell coffee?”'
    body: |
      Harvey’s dad, John Uyeyama, once owned a coffee shop called Lafayette Coffee Roasters. When it closed, he kept the roaster and kept roasting at home.

      “He learned on the same machine that’s behind me now,” Harvey told ABC10.

      “We would never go to the store for coffee,” Harvey told The Davis Enterprise.

      Harvey had other business ideas. One was painting house numbers on curbs.

      His dad did not like it. He told Harvey to find a business that could last.

      One night in June 2024, they roasted together from about 11 p.m. to 1 a.m. Afterward, over coffee in the kitchen, his dad had an idea.

      “Why don’t you just sell coffee? We have coffee in the house. I can teach you how to roast coffee. You can bag it up, you can go around the neighborhood selling coffee,” his dad said, as Harvey told the Enterprise.

      It beat painting curbs.

  - type: prose
    heading: "Too good for a funeral"
    body: |
      Then came August, and Harvey’s 15th birthday.

      “I received a call from my mom that my dad was unconscious, on the side of the road…” Harvey told ABC10.

      His dad had a heart attack. He died that day.

      “I went outside for a second, looked at the coffee roaster, because coffee was like our one bonding thing,” Harvey told the station.

      Weeks later, the church needed coffee for the funeral. Harvey had never roasted alone. That night, he roasted about 50 bags.

      People called it “too good coffee for a funeral,” Harvey told the Bee.

      “And I was like, huh, maybe that’s a sign,” he said.

      He decided to keep going. As he told ABC10, “this is part of his legacy, and this could be part of my legacy.”

      So the coffee is roasted first, before anyone knows who will drink it.

      Then the bag waits. In a truck. In a warehouse. In the back of a store. And finally, in your cupboard.

      Coffee starts to lose its freshness almost right after it is roasted. The National Coffee Association says so.

      None of that is lying. It is just what happens when your customer is a shelf.

      So what would coffee look like if the customer were a person?

      Roasted after you order it. From good farms. Made to fit the way you brew.

  - type: prose
    heading: "Their answer: no shelf"
    body: |
      At Harvey’s Coffee, nothing gets roasted until you order it. Then it’s roasted, boxed, and shipped. There is no shelf.

      The company says its beans come from the top 1% of farms, hand-picked.

      A short quiz matches your coffee to how you brew.

      Then Harvey roasts it on his dad’s old machine. The Bee compared its sound to an old train engine.

      He turns the flames up and down by hand. He listens for the “crack,” a sound the beans make as they roast.

      “We want a roaster that’s manual, you need to have a human sitting in front of it,” he told the Bee.
